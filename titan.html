A watch company, a state institution and forty years of compounding

Titan: More Than a Tata Success Story

Tata built the business. Tamil Nadu helped build the institutional runway. The interesting lesson is not who deserves all the credit, but how private execution and public policy became mutually reinforcing.

I began thinking about Titan after watching Made in India: A Titan Story. The series is effective because it gives industrial history the pace of a boardroom drama: J.R.D. Tata makes a bold offer, a sceptical minister asks why Tamil Nadu should hand land to capitalists, and officials insist that the project must create local employment and public value. It is memorable television. It is also a reminder to separate three things that are often merged into one story: what the series dramatises, what the historical record establishes, and what we can reasonably infer from the outcome.

Titan is neither simply a Tata triumph nor simply a TIDCO triumph. It is a case in which each side supplied something the other could not easily produce alone.

Why this story matters now

Most corporate histories are written as stories of founders, products and markets. Public policy appears only as background. Titan invites a different reading because the policy architecture is still visible in its ownership. TIDCO—the Tamil Nadu Industrial Development Corporation—remains a promoter and holds 27.88% of Titan. Titan’s own investor material still describes the company as a joint venture between the Tata Group and TIDCO.

That does not mean TIDCO designed the watches, built the retail network or created Tanishq. Those achievements came from Titan’s managers, engineers, designers, workers, dealers and the wider Tata system. But it does mean the state was not merely a landlord collecting rent. It took equity, accepted risk and retained a formal place in governance.

The policy context: why a joint sector existed at all

Titan was born before the 1991 reforms, when industrial entry was shaped by licensing, foreign-exchange controls, technology approvals and concern about the concentration of economic power. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 created a licensing framework for new industrial undertakings. The MRTP regime added another layer of scrutiny for large business houses. In that environment, state industrial-development corporations emerged as institutional partners: they could identify projects, provide land and facilitation, take minority equity and represent public objectives without attempting to run every factory themselves.

TIDCO describes its own purpose in precisely this language: it was established in 1965 to promote medium and large industries in association with the private sector. Its joint-sector model was not created for Titan alone. Tamil Nadu used similar structures in fertilisers, chemicals, petrochemicals, industrial parks and infrastructure.

Seen in this wider context, Titan was not a clever loophole invented solely to evade the MRTP Act. It was one application of a recognised mixed-economy instrument. The regulation created friction, but the joint sector also created a bargaining space in which a state could ask for equity, board representation, location commitments and employment benefits.

The famous 27.88% offer: powerful scene, incomplete archive

One of the series’ strongest moments has J.R.D. Tata offering TIDCO 27.88% equity and linking the project to employment for people from Tamil Nadu. The dialogue captures the economic logic beautifully: the state would not merely give away land; it would become a shareholder in an industry intended to create local capability.

But the exact conversation should be treated as dramatisation unless supported by a contemporaneous letter, board minute, cabinet note or signed agreement. I have not found a publicly accessible official record that reproduces the scene word for word. What official records do establish is that Titan was incorporated in 1984 as a Tata Industries–TIDCO joint venture and that TIDCO now records a 27.88% holding.

What can be stated confidently

  • Titan was established as a joint venture between Tata Industries and TIDCO.
  • TIDCO’s present holding is 27.88%, according to TIDCO’s annual return and Titan’s shareholding disclosures.
  • TIDCO nominees have held the chairperson’s role, while Titan’s managing leadership has come through the operating company and Tata side.
  • The Hosur manufacturing base created a durable industrial and employment footprint.

What should be labelled as dramatisation or interpretation

  • The exact minister–J.R.D. exchange shown in the series.
  • The claim that one individual official personally designed every governance safeguard.
  • The idea that TIDCO alone “created” Titan, or that Tata merely supplied money.

Governance was the real design

The arrangement worked because public participation did not require day-to-day bureaucratic management. Titan’s later governance records show the continuing pattern: a TIDCO nominee can serve as chairperson, while Tata nominees and professional executives participate in the board and management. In the 2025–26 annual report, for example, Sandhya Sharma, an IAS officer and TIDCO nominee, was appointed chairperson, while Ajoy Chawla became managing director.

This division matters. The state retained visibility and ownership; the company retained operating autonomy. The best joint-sector arrangements are not those in which government and business compete to control every decision. They are those in which the public partner protects the long-term bargain and the operating team is judged on execution.

What the public partner contributed

Institutional legitimacy, project facilitation, equity participation, state coordination, a long-term local-development interest and board-level presence.

What the private partner contributed

Management, product strategy, technology relationships, brand building, distribution, retail execution and the willingness to keep investing through failure.

Hosur was not incidental

The Titan factory at Hosur placed the company inside Tamil Nadu’s industrial geography while keeping it close to Bengaluru’s engineering and management ecosystem. Titan’s official heritage page dates the Hosur factory inauguration to 1988 and places J.R.D. Tata and Xerxes Desai at the centre of the event.

The deeper effect was capability formation. A factory does not create development merely because it occupies land. It matters when it builds technical skills, supplier relationships, disciplined production systems and career pathways. Titan became known for recruiting and training people from the region, including women from rural communities. The series gives this theme emotional weight. The broader historical point is credible even when individual scenes are dramatised: the project linked a consumer brand to a manufacturing workforce that was not drawn only from India’s metropolitan elite.

This is where the financial and social sides of policy meet. Local employment was not charity detached from business. A trained, stable workforce was part of the productive asset. Tamil Nadu gained jobs, capabilities and equity value; Titan gained labour, location, legitimacy and an industrial base.

Who benefited—and how?

The partnership was mutually beneficial, but not in identical ways.

Titan and the Tata group

Titan received an institutional route into a regulated industry, a manufacturing location, a state partner and the conditions to build at scale. Over time, the company created some of India’s strongest consumer brands. The operational and reputational gains to Tata were far larger than the original watch project.

Tamil Nadu and TIDCO

Tamil Nadu gained industrial activity, employment and a continuing ownership position. The 27.88% stake became extraordinarily valuable because TIDCO did not exit during Titan’s later growth. The public return is therefore not only the current market value of the shares. It also includes decades of wages, supplier activity, taxes, skills and the signalling effect of a globally recognised company manufacturing from the state.

Employees and communities

Employees gained access to organised industrial work, training and mobility. The benefits were not perfectly distributed, and one company cannot solve rural inequality. Yet the project illustrates how equity policy and employment policy can reinforce each other when the underlying enterprise becomes productive.

The underrated decision: not selling

Much of the recent public discussion celebrates TIDCO for holding its stake. That conclusion is understandable, but hindsight can make patience look effortless. In 2002, TIDCO was publicly considering whether to participate in a possible Titan rights issue, which could have diluted its holding. The fact that the stake remains at 27.88% today reflects not a single moment of foresight but a series of decisions across governments and officials.

Holding a successful asset can create enormous public wealth. Holding every state investment forever would not. Many joint-sector companies failed, were sold or became financially distressed. The policy lesson is not “government must never sell.” It is that the state needs the capacity to distinguish a strategic, well-governed compounding asset from a project whose original purpose has ended.

Titan was exceptional, but the model was not unique

Other states used similar structures with very different outcomes. The comparison prevents us from turning Titan into a morality play in which one state discovered a magic formula.

What makes Titan unusual is the combination of four outcomes: strong private execution, durable public ownership, a successful consumer brand and continued formal partnership. In many other cases the state sold, the private partner absorbed the company, or the project became distressed.

What this story should not be used to claim

It would be wrong to use Titan as proof that every state-owned stake is wise, every public–private venture is efficient or every industrial licence improved development. The pre-1991 system also produced delays, scarcity, rent-seeking and protection from competition. HMT’s earlier role in creating India’s watchmaking capability should not disappear simply because Titan later outperformed it.

It would also be wrong to reduce Titan to a licence transaction. A licence can allow entry; it cannot create forty years of product development, marketing, retail discipline and reinvention. Tanishq’s eventual success, for example, came after costly experimentation and years of learning. TIDCO shared in the upside because it remained an owner, not because it operated the jewellery business.

The policy lesson I take from Titan

The strongest lesson is not that the state should run companies, nor that the state should disappear. It is that government can sometimes create value by choosing the right level of involvement.

  1. Take risk when markets and capabilities are missing. A development corporation can help a serious project cross the gap between idea and investment.
  2. Negotiate public value rather than distribute concessions. Land, approvals and infrastructure should be linked to equity, jobs, technology or measurable commitments.
  3. Protect governance without micromanaging operations. Board representation and transparency matter; everyday commercial decisions need professional management.
  4. Build local capability, not only physical assets. Training, suppliers and employment pathways make an investment regionally meaningful.
  5. Know when to hold and when to exit. Public ownership should be reviewed strategically, not ideologically.

The enduring achievement was not that Tamil Nadu “beat” Tata, or that Tata outsmarted regulation. It was that a public institution and a private enterprise found a structure in which both could gain from the same company becoming productive.
