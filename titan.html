A watch company, a state institution and forty years of compounding

Titan: More Than a Tata Success Story

Tata built the business. Tamil Nadu helped build the institutional runway. The useful lesson is how private execution and public policy reinforced each other.

I began thinking about Titan after watching Made in India: A Titan Story. The series turns industrial history into a boardroom drama: J.R.D. Tata makes a bold offer, a minister questions why Tamil Nadu should give land to capitalists, and officials demand local employment and public value. It is compelling television. It is also a reason to separate dramatisation from documented history.

Titan was not simply a Tata triumph or a TIDCO triumph. Each side supplied something the other could not easily produce alone.

Why this story matters

Corporate histories usually centre founders, products and markets. Titan invites a wider reading because public policy is still visible in its ownership. TIDCO, the Tamil Nadu Industrial Development Corporation, remains a promoter and holds 27.88% of Titan Company.

This does not mean TIDCO designed watches, created Tanishq or built the retail network. Those achievements came from Titan's managers, engineers, designers, workers, dealers and the wider Tata system. But the state was not merely a landlord. It invested equity, accepted risk and retained a place in governance.

Why a joint sector existed

Titan was born before the 1991 reforms, when industrial entry was shaped by licensing, foreign exchange controls, technology approvals and concern about concentrated economic power. State development corporations emerged as institutional partners. They could identify projects, provide land and facilitation, take minority equity and represent public objectives without trying to run every factory.

TIDCO was created in 1965 to promote medium and large industries with private partners. Titan was one use of that broader model, not a special loophole invented only for Tata. The structure gave the state bargaining space to seek equity, board representation, location commitments and employment benefits.

The famous 27.88% scene: drama versus record

The series shows J.R.D. Tata offering TIDCO 27.88% equity while linking the project to employment in Tamil Nadu. The scene captures the economic logic well: the state would not simply transfer land; it would become a shareholder in a productive enterprise.

The exact dialogue, however, should be treated as dramatisation unless supported by a letter, board minute, cabinet note or signed agreement. The official record confirms the 1984 Tata Industries and TIDCO joint venture and TIDCO's 27.88% holding. It does not, in the material publicly available to me, reproduce the scene word for word.

Documented

The real design was governance

The arrangement worked because public participation did not require daily bureaucratic control. The state retained ownership and visibility, while the operating company retained professional management. Titan's 2025–26 annual report, for example, records a TIDCO nominee as chairperson and a professional managing director leading operations.

Public partner

Equity, legitimacy, project facilitation, state coordination, local-development interest and board-level presence.

Private partner

Management, technology relationships, product strategy, branding, distribution, retail execution and continued investment.

Why Hosur mattered

The Hosur factory placed Titan inside Tamil Nadu's industrial geography while keeping it close to Bengaluru's engineering and management ecosystem. A factory creates lasting value when it builds skills, suppliers, production discipline and careers. Titan's recruitment and training of people from the region, including women from rural communities, became part of that capability-building story.

Local employment was not charity detached from business. A trained and stable workforce was a productive asset. Tamil Nadu gained jobs, capabilities and equity value. Titan gained labour, location, legitimacy and an industrial base.

Who benefited, and how?

Titan received an institutional route into a regulated industry, a manufacturing location, a state partner and the conditions to build at scale. Over time it created strong consumer brands far beyond watches.

Tamil Nadu gained industrial activity, employment and a continuing ownership position. The public return includes not only the value of TIDCO's stake, but also wages, suppliers, taxes, skills and the signalling effect of a globally recognised company manufacturing from the state.

Employees gained organised industrial work, training and mobility. The gains were not perfectly distributed, and one company cannot solve rural inequality, but the project shows how employment policy and equity policy can reinforce each other.

The underrated decision: holding the stake

TIDCO's decision to retain its stake is now widely celebrated. Hindsight makes that patience look easy. In reality, it reflects repeated choices across governments and officials. Holding a successful asset can create enormous public wealth, but holding every state investment forever would not. The real skill is distinguishing a well-governed compounding asset from a project whose original purpose has ended.

Titan was exceptional, not unique

Other states used similar structures with different outcomes. The comparison prevents Titan from becoming a morality play in which one state discovered a magic formula.

The policy lesson

Titan does not prove that every state-owned stake is wise or every public-private venture is efficient. The pre-1991 system also produced delays, scarcity and protection from competition. A licence could allow entry, but it could not create decades of product development, branding, retail discipline and reinvention.

  1. Take risk where capabilities are missing. Development institutions can help a serious project cross the gap between idea and investment.
  2. Negotiate public value. Land and facilitation should be linked to equity, jobs, technology or measurable commitments.
  3. Protect governance without micromanaging. Board representation matters; daily commercial decisions need professional management.
  4. Build local capability. Training, suppliers and employment pathways matter more than the factory building alone.
  5. Review ownership strategically. Holding and exiting are both policy choices, not ideological rules.

The enduring achievement was not that Tamil Nadu beat Tata, or that Tata outsmarted regulation. It was that a public institution and a private enterprise both gained from the same company becoming productive.
